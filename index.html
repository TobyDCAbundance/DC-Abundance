How we think about abundance

A lens, not a faction

Much of what makes life expensive or out of reach is not scarce because of nature or because of money. It is scarce because of choices we made about how to build, who gets to decide, and how long deciding should take.

That question travels — across housing, transportation, energy, healthcare, childcare and science. Some answers read as left, some as right, and the interesting ones usually read as neither.

What we do

We host events with one specific purpose: getting people who are interested in abundance actually engaging with abundance ideas, across the full spectrum of abundance policy. That means housing one month and energy the next, healthcare or childcare after that. The point is not to specialise, it is to keep the frame moving across the areas where it has purchase.

DC is full of people working on these questions in different lanes, and it has remarkably few rooms where those people meet each other. We make those rooms.

We are genuinely open to collaboration. If you have an idea for an event, a speaker you want to hear from, or someone you would like an introduction to, tell us. A good share of what we have run started as a suggestion from someone we met at a happy hour.

How we convene

Three kinds of event

  • 01

    Educational events

    Our bread and butter so far. We bring in experts from abundance think tanks, policy organizations and academia to talk about their projects, their ideas, and the organizations they are building. These are the nights you leave knowing something specific you did not know before.

  • 02

    Engagement-building events

    Still an experiment. The goal is to get members thinking about and doing abundance work themselves rather than only listening to it. The format that has worked best is Lightning Talks: five-minute talks from our own community on anything abundance-adjacent. You do not have to be an expert, you just need a good idea — and it is the kind of thing that can go on a resume.

  • 03

    Social events

    The happy hour is the format we keep coming back to, and we expect to run one monthly. We also want to do looser things: abundance third spaces, rooftop parties, house parties. Less academic, same people, and usually where the useful introductions actually happen.

What Abundance Means to Us

We view abundance as a frame through which a whole swath of policy problems can be viewed.

The basic formula starts by asking two questions:

Part one

The things we need more of

Housing

Housing is expensive because it's illegal to build more of it

Housing costs have climbed for decades, and the worst of it is in the places with the most jobs. We haven't built enough to keep up with demand. That raises the cost of living, and it makes it harder for people to move somewhere with better work.

Two things drive the shortage. In most of the country, zoning rules make it illegal to build the kind of housing people actually need. And in the places where building is legal, opponents use lawsuits and discretionary review to stall projects until they're too expensive to finish.

Housing is the most developed piece of the abundance agenda, mostly thanks to a decade of YIMBY organizing in cities across the country.

Transportation

Transportation costs so much to build that American cities can't afford what's standard elsewhere

Transportation and housing are the same problem from a different angle. Good transit widens the ring of land people can live on and still reach job-rich places.

Our system lags behind peer countries mainly because building here costs up to three times more. Cost decides what gets built, which is a big part of why we have so few new metro lines and no high-speed rail. The usual culprits: weaponized legal review, overreliance on consultants hired to do work the government no longer has the in-house talent to do, and procurement rules that reward the lowest bid over reliable delivery.

Energy

Cheap, abundant energy is already possible. Rules block the infrastructure.

The price of energy shows up in nearly everything people pay for. It sets what it costs to cool a house and it's baked into the price of groceries. Cost and reliability also decide where job-creating industries decide to locate.

Demand for power is only going up. Solar and wind now cost less than fossil fuels in most of the country, without the same environmental damage.

The bottleneck is physical infrastructure. Building it at the scale we need means clearing overlapping permitting rules, legal reviews, and local approvals, each of which adds delay. No energy source escapes this, but transmission has it worst, because lines cross jurisdictions and need a yes from each.

Science

We're spending more on science and getting less back

Technological progress has driven rising living standards since the industrial revolution. Lately we're getting fewer big breakthroughs per dollar spent.

Part of that is how science gets funded in the US. Grantmakers reward projects likely to produce a result, even a small one, over ambitious projects that might fail but change everything, like mRNA technology. Applications also take longer than they used to, so scientists spend a growing share of their careers writing them instead of doing the work.

Discovery is only half of it. A breakthrough only helps anyone once someone finds a place to build it, manufactures it at scale, connects it to the grid, and gets all of that approved. Every one of those steps has a gatekeeper who can hold up the benefits for years.

Health

Healthcare is expensive because we decided to produce less of it

Americans pay more for healthcare than any comparable country and get worse outcomes. Most of the debate is about subsidizing the price of care. The abundance argument is that you can also bring the price down by increasing the volume of care and medicine.

Supply is constricted, often on purpose. Medicare-funded residency slots have been capped near 1996 levels, which limits how many new doctors we get. Certificate of Need laws let existing hospitals block competitors from entering their market. Scope of practice rules stop nurse practitioners and pharmacists from doing work they're trained to do. A small group of providers set prices.

Reformers point to Operation Warp Speed as proof that approval timelines can be cut without cutting corners, and argue that should be the standard rather than an emergency measure.

Childcare

Childcare is expensive partly because opening a daycare is harder than it needs to be

For families across the country, childcare now costs as much as rent or more. Caring for children is labor intensive, so cost-cutting is difficult. Most families will need help paying for it.

New Mexico shows why subsidies aren't the whole answer. It became the first state to offer free childcare to every family. However, the state is short more than 15,000 seats for kids under six.

When there aren't enough spots, the ones that exist go to the parents who have a car and can drive across town, take an afternoon off to tour a center, and figure out which waitlists to get on. Everyone else keeps their kid home or relies on informal care.

There aren't enough spots because opening a daycare is harder than it needs to be. Zoning bans formal daycare centers from most residential neighborhoods. Fire codes and staffing rules contradict each other. Each rule had a reason. Together they make it slow and expensive to open the slots to use a subsidy on.

Part two

Why we don't have more of these things

State capacity

Government has lost the capacity to do what we ask of it

We argue constantly about what government should do and not enough about whether it can do what we've tasked it to do.

Government projects run late and over budget. The people running it mean well but are working inside systems built for a different era. An agency that needs engineers must spend months in a hiring process or hire a consulting firm with its own bottom line. Public servants get rewarded for following the process even when it produces a bad outcome, and punished for deviating even when it produces a good one. The government views software as something to be bought rather than built iteratively, which has wasted hundreds of millions of dollars. Much of this stems from the lack of engagement between the people who write policy and the people who implement it.

Most of these rules were put in place to stop fraud and favoritism. Now they prevent the government from fixing bridges or making healthcare sign-up websites.

This isn't an argument for more government or less of it. It's that we'd have more of what we need if government could do the things we need it to do.

Artificial scarcity

Some scarcity is written into the law

Some things are scarce because they're hard to make. Others are scarce because you need permission to make them, and the people already in the business help decide who gets it.

In about 30 states, opening a clinic means proving that the area needs one. The hospitals already there can lobby to argue it doesn't. It's not only businesses: in Napa, where there are ten babies for every daycare spot, neighbors sued to stop a daycare from expanding into a church building.

These rules usually started with a real purpose. What they sometimes do now is protect whoever got there first. The people who benefit fight hard to keep those rules.

The people who pay higher prices for medical care, roof repair, or haircuts rarely know the rule exists. They just see their lives get more expensive until they must leave the neighborhood they grew up in or switch to a doctor far from their home.
